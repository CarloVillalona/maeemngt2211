About the course

A master's course in the MAEd in Educational Management that asks each student to diagnose one real institution and design a workable plan to improve it.

Leadership in Education critically examines contemporary theories, research, policies, and practices of educational leadership and their application to the management and transformation of educational institutions.

It develops graduate students' capacity to exercise ethical, research-informed, strategic, instructional, inclusive, innovative, and sustainable leadership in diverse educational contexts.

Following the approved syllabus, the course covers ten topics: leadership foundations; strategic and organizational development; instructional and curriculum leadership; organizational culture and change; governance and policy; resource and financial stewardship; human capital; legal and ethical leadership; digital transformation and responsible AI; and stakeholder and community engagement.

Because this is a master's course for working teachers and school leaders, students do more than summarize theories. They analyze authentic institutional issues, educational data, policies, and scholarly literature, and use that evidence to make leadership decisions that fit their own setting. The course culminates in a School Leadership Improvement Portfolio that brings a diagnosis, a strategy, a people plan, a budget, and an engagement plan together around one priority.

Why it matters

Research places school leadership among the strongest school-level influences on learning: an effective principal's effect on achievement is nearly as large as an effective teacher's, and it reaches every classroom in the school (Grissom et al., 2021). Republic Act No. 9155 asks the Philippine school head to be “both an instructional leader and administrative manager.” Students in this program are the teachers, coordinators, and school heads who carry that work.

What sets it apart

The syllabus lists more than twenty separate outputs across ten topics. In this four-week module they are consolidated into four weekly products that build on one another and on one real institution, so each student finishes with a single coherent plan and not many unconnected papers.

How the course thinks

The course works at three levels throughout, and moves in a deliberate sequence from theory to reflection. Every step is taken on the same institution.

Level 1

Understand

What do theory, research, and the Philippine standards say good school leadership is, and what do they say it does for learners?

Level 2

Diagnose

What does the evidence from my own institution show, what does it hide, and what one priority does it point to?

Level 3

Design and lead

What plan would move that priority, who must carry it, what will it cost, and how will it stay lawful, fair, and accountable?

October 17, 24, and 31: conference-style reporting

The last three Saturdays run as a small academic conference. Each of the eight groups has its own session: the professor frames the topic in a 15-minute lecture, then every member presents alone for 10 minutes on an assigned inquiry question, with up to 5 slides, and takes 5 minutes of follow-up questions. The group shares a topic and a reporting day, not a presentation grade.

Group 1Session 1 · Oct 17 · 4 members

Leading Organizational Culture, Change, and Innovation

Organizational culture, change leadership, innovation leadership, conflict management, crisis leadership.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. Conduct a brief culture audit of a school you know: what people see and do, what the school says it values, and what is simply taken for granted. Where is the widest gap between the stated values and actual practice, and what should a leader do first to narrow it?
  2. Fullan (2021) contrasts “right” and “wrong” drivers of system change. Which wrong driver do you see most clearly in a recent change in your school or division, and what would the corresponding right driver look like there?
  3. Why do teachers resist changes they privately agree with? Take one recent change in your institution and show how a leader could have carried it through without breaking trust.
  4. When a school is disrupted, by a typhoon and prolonged class suspension or by open conflict among faculty, what separates leading through the crisis from merely managing it, and what should already be in place before the crisis comes?
Group 2Session 2 · Oct 17 · 4 members

Educational Governance, Policy, and Systems Leadership

Governance structures, educational policies, school-based management, educational reforms.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. Republic Act No. 9155 gives the school head “authority, accountability and responsibility” for the school. In practice, how much real decision-making room does a public school head, or a private school principal, have, and where exactly does it end?
  2. Is school-based management in the Philippines genuine decentralization or delegated paperwork? Use a School Governance Council or a School Improvement Plan process you know as your evidence.
  3. Choose one finding of the Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM II). At which level does responsibility for it lie (national, division, or school), and what can a school leader do about it without waiting for higher offices?
  4. When a national or division policy does not fit local conditions, should a school leader comply, adapt, or push back? Use one concrete policy to show how a leader can adapt responsibly while remaining accountable.
Group 3Session 3 · Oct 17 · 3 members

Strategic Resource and Financial Leadership

Educational finance, budget planning, resource allocation, financial accountability, grant management.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. With a fixed school MOOE or department budget and several competing needs, what criteria should decide how the money is allocated, and who should take part in the decision?
  2. Transparency boards and School Report Cards are required of schools. Do they produce real accountability? What would make a school's financial reporting meaningful to parents and teachers?
  3. Schools draw on PTA contributions, the local government's Special Education Fund, donations, and grants. What are the ethical and practical risks of relying on outside resources, and how should a leader manage them?
Group 4Session 4 · Oct 24 · 4 members

Human Capital Leadership and Talent Development

Human resource management and recruitment, performance management, professional development, succession planning.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. The PIDS study by David, Albert, and Vizmanos (2019) documents the weight of non-teaching tasks on public school teachers. As a school leader with no additional staff, what could you realistically take off teachers' plates, and how would you decide?
  2. Does the performance management system in your institution develop teachers or merely rate them? What would have to change in how a school head uses it for it to improve teaching?
  3. Darling-Hammond, Hyler, and Gardner (2017) identify seven features of effective professional development. Evaluate one LAC session or in-service training you attended against them, and redesign it.
  4. When a strong school head retires or is transferred, improvement often stops. How should a school identify and prepare its next leaders, given the hiring and promotion rules it must follow?
Group 5Session 5 · Oct 24 · 4 members

Educational Law for School Leaders

The Education Act of 1982, the Magna Carta for Public School Teachers, child protection, data privacy, inclusive education law.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. A parent demands that a teacher be disciplined for a classroom incident that has been posted online. What do the teacher's right to due process and the school's duty to protect the child each require, and what should the school head do in the first 48 hours?
  2. A teacher posts class photos and a list of grades in a group chat; an outside organization asks the school for learner records. What does the Data Privacy Act of 2012 require in each case, and which everyday habits in schools commonly violate it?
  3. A learner with a disability is refused enrollment because the school “lacks facilities.” What does Republic Act No. 11650 require of the school, and what should a leader do when the resources truly are lacking?
  4. The Education Act of 1982 sets out the rights and duties of students, parents, teachers, and school administrators. Where do these rights most often collide in everyday school decisions, and how should a leader resolve the collision?
Group 6Session 6 · Oct 24 · 3 members

Ethical Leadership, Equity, and Inclusion

Ethical leadership, ethical dilemmas and decision-making, equity, inclusive and non-discriminatory practice.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. A superior instructs you to do something that is permitted but, in your judgment, unfair to learners, for example promoting all learners to improve the school's statistics. How should you reason about it, and what should you actually do?
  2. You discover that a close colleague has altered grades or misused school funds. In a workplace shaped by pakikisama and utang na loob, what does ethical leadership require, and how can it be done justly?
  3. Policies that look neutral can disadvantage particular learners: fees, uniform rules, device requirements, class schedules. Identify one such policy in a school you know and redesign it so that it is fair.
Group 7Session 7 · Oct 31 · 4 members

Digital Transformation and Innovation Leadership in Education

Digital leadership, AI in education, technology integration, digital governance, cybersecurity.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. Conduct a short technology audit of your school. Which digital tools have actually improved teaching or management, and which have only added work? What should the leader stop, keep, and start?
  2. Should a school allow teachers and learners to use generative AI? Draft the core of a school-level policy that is consistent with DepEd's guidance on AI and with the Data Privacy Act of 2012.
  3. How should a leader pursue digital transformation in a school where many learners and teachers lack devices and reliable connectivity, without widening the gap between those who have and those who have not?
  4. Who is responsible for learner data in a school (enrollment records, grades, health and family information), and what minimum safeguards should a school head put in place against loss, leakage, and misuse?
Group 8Session 8 · Oct 31 · 3 members

Community and Stakeholder Engagement, and Course Synthesis

Community building, stakeholder engagement and communication, partnership development, course synthesis.

Inquiry questions, one per member
  1. Mapp and Kuttner (2013) argue that families and school staff both need capabilities, connections, confidence, and the right beliefs to work together. Evaluate one PTA or Brigada Eskwela activity you know: does it build capacity, or does it only collect contributions? Redesign it.
  2. Schools enter partnerships with local government, alumni, businesses, and donors, including under the Adopt-a-School Act. What makes a partnership serve learning and not publicity, and when should a leader decline a partner?
  3. As the closing presenter of the course: synthesize the ten topics into a single, defensible statement of what responsible educational leadership should look like in a Philippine school today, including your position on the role of AI.

Twelve guiding questions

Students return to these questions throughout the four weeks.

  1. What distinguishes leadership from management, and when does a school need more of one than of the other?
  2. Which leadership theories actually explain what effective leaders do in Philippine schools?
  3. Through what pathways does a leader influence learning in classrooms that the leader does not teach?
  4. How do we know what our school most needs: what do the data say, and what do they hide?
  5. Why do improvement plans so often stay on paper?
  6. How is a school's culture formed, and how can it be changed without breaking trust?
  7. Who really decides in Philippine education, and how much room does a school leader have?
  8. How should scarce resources be allocated, and how do we show the community that they were used well?
  9. How do leaders grow teachers and future leaders, and not merely evaluate them?
  10. What should a leader do when law, ethics, and loyalty pull in different directions?
  11. What is the responsible role of artificial intelligence and digital tools in leading a school?
  12. What kind of leader am I becoming, and for whom?

The Philippine framework for school leadership, 1966 to 2026

The laws, department orders, and reports that define what a Philippine school leader may do, must do, and is asked to answer for. All are read in their original text in the course.

  1. 1966
    Republic Act No. 4670, Magna Carta for Public School TeachersSets out the rights, working conditions, and safeguards of public school teachers, including due process in administrative cases.
  2. 1982
    Batas Pambansa Blg. 232, Education Act of 1982States the rights and duties of students, parents, teachers, and school administrators, the ground on which most everyday school disputes are settled.
  3. 1998
    Republic Act No. 8525, Adopt-a-School ActOpens public schools to assistance from private entities, the legal basis for many school partnerships.
  4. 2001
    Republic Act No. 9155, Governance of Basic Education ActEstablishes shared governance in basic education and describes the school head as “both an instructional leader and administrative manager.”
  5. 2012
    Republic Act No. 10173, Data Privacy ActGoverns how schools collect, keep, share, and dispose of the personal information of learners, parents, and staff.
  6. 2014
    DepEd Order No. 12, on school MOOEGuidelines on the direct release of Maintenance and Other Operating Expenses allocations to schools, later revised in 2019.
  7. 2015
    DepEd Order No. 44, the enhanced School Improvement PlanSets the school improvement planning process and the School Report Card.
  8. 2016
    DepEd Order No. 35, the Learning Action CellMakes the LAC the school-based strategy for teachers' continuing professional development.
  9. 2020
    DepEd Order No. 24, Philippine Professional Standards for School HeadsDefines what school heads are expected to know and do across five domains and four career stages. The course reads its ten topics against these domains.
  10. 2022
    Republic Act No. 11650, on inclusive educationInstitutes a policy of inclusion and services for learners with disabilities.
  11. 2024–26
    Second Congressional Commission on Education (EDCOM II) reportsMiseducation, Fixing the Foundations, and Turning Point document the condition of Philippine education that today's school leaders are asked to respond to.

Academic integrity and AI

The sources you cite must exist and say what you claim, and the analysis must be yours.

  • Disclose. Add a short note at the end of each paper naming any AI tool you used and what you used it for.
  • Support, not replace. AI may help you brainstorm, check language, or organize notes. The analysis, the data, and the reflection must be your own.
  • No invented evidence. Invented data or references are treated as academic dishonesty.

Confidentiality

When you write or present about your own institution:

  • Use only data you are permitted to access.
  • Remove the names of learners, colleagues, parents, and officials. Give the school a neutral label if naming it could identify individuals.
  • Do not enter anyone's personal information into an AI tool or a public website.
